16 · Drew Eric Whitman, Cashvertising (LF8 and learned wants) · see also Maslow’s hierarchy of needs (1943)

Yes, the best insights start from a human need or want, but the need alone is data: everyone wants to be safe. The insight is the specific tension between that need and what's in the way for THIS audience. Dove (previous slide) sits on LF8 no. 8, social approval, blocked by self-criticism; it's tagged here. Whitman's argument: the eight biological desires beat the learned wants, so anchor a learned want (convenience) to an LF8 (more time with the people you love). Other lenses if asked: Maslow (physiological, safety, belonging, esteem, self-actualisation); Deci and Ryan's Self-Determination Theory (autonomy, competence, relatedness). Exercise: for our funny brand, pick one LF8, then find the tension.

How Facebook decides who sees your ad

On Facebook, the creative is the targeting.

01

Sales or Leads. Facebook optimises for exactly what you ask for, so ask for the result you want, not clicks.

02

24 · Check Meta Business Help Centre for current guidance

The big shift in Facebook ads: the algorithm now does most of the targeting, and it reads your creative to decide who to show it to (Meta's newer retrieval system, Andromeda, leans on this heavily). A pillow ad about snoring partners finds couples; one about night shifts finds nurses. So the brand kit and insights decide your audience as much as any targeting setting. Practical rules: choose Sales or Leads as the campaign objective, use broad audiences and Advantage+ placements, and give each ad set several genuinely different ads rather than one ad with ten button colours. Statics are the cheapest way to supply that variety. Set up the Pixel and Conversions API before spending. Platform behaviour changes often: check Meta's current help pages before the session.

30 · Ads Live Build · Gareth Winter

Angles (previous slide) are the reason to care; narratives are the shape of the ad. Any angle can wear any narrative, which is how three angles become many ads. PAS and Before-After-Bridge are the workhorses of direct response. Hook, Story, Offer is Russell Brunson's. Customer-as-hero is Donald Miller's StoryBrand: the customer is Luke, you are Yoda. Pope in the Pool is from Blake Snyder's Save the Cat: when you have to deliver dull information, put something entertaining on screen at the same time (his example: exposition delivered while the Pope swims laps). Listicle and myth-busting are Hook Point formats (perspective shift is one of their performance drivers). Controversy: attack a belief or a category habit, never a person. For story-led ones, layer HSTSS on top. Live: pick one angle for our brand and run it through three narratives. Facebook warning for Problem, Agitate, Solve: agitating the pain is where most policy rejections happen, because it tempts you to address the viewer's problem directly ('You're exhausted and broke'). Agitate the situation, not the person: 'Most budgets fail by the 12th of the month.'

32 · Ads Live Build · Gareth Winter

The horse-racing logic: each round is a race with one variable. The winner of each race carries forward and everything else is locked. Round 1 tests the message (the angle), because the message matters most and is cheapest to change. Round 2 keeps the winning headline and tests the visual. Round 3 keeps both and tests the placement or format. Only then do we spend on video, which is backing the horse that has already won three races. Rules for each race: same audience, same budget, same dates; pick the metric (usually cost per result) before launch; give it enough spend to judge; stop the losers. The 3×3 matrix on the previous slide is the same idea run in parallel; if budget is small, run it as these sequential rounds instead. Facebook rule of thumb: an ad set needs roughly 50 results (purchases or leads) in a week to leave the learning phase, so budget each round for that or judge on a cheaper earlier metric such as cost per landing-page view. Don't touch a running test.
